Shade by votes, not by percentages

The usual election map shades each county by its percentage: the bigger the win, the darker the color. That makes a county where a hundred people voted look just as decisive as a big city. Vote-lead color shades each county by how many votes its lead was worth instead, so places that cast few votes fade out.

1

The usual map

Each county is colored for whoever won it, and shaded by the winner's share: light for a close race, dark for a landslide.

2

Where it fails: a tiny landslide looks bigger than a city

A small county that votes one way almost unanimously gets the darkest shade, darker than a big city that won by a huge number of votes.

3

Where it fails: dark ink, few votes

The darkest counties are mostly the emptiest. They cover a lot of the map but cast few of its votes.

4

Where it fails: a big, close county looks like nothing

A close race gets the lightest shade, even when the county is so big that a narrow win is worth tens of thousands of votes.

5

Vote-lead color

Now each county is shaded by its lead in votes: the winner's votes minus the runner-up's. The more votes a county added to its winner's statewide total, the darker it is.

The shading is on a square-root scale, the way a circle's area is scaled on a bubble map, so a county with a quarter of the biggest lead is half as dark. Without it, every county but a handful would be white.

6

Now the city outweighs the tiny landslide

The small county fades out, and the city is among the darkest on the map.

7

The empty counties fade

The counties that were the darkest red now barely show, because together they added few votes.

8

What vote-lead color hides

No color scale shows everything. This one leaves out how a county split.

  • A pale county may still have voted 9 to 1. It just didn't have many voters. Slide back to see its percentage.
  • A big county can be dark with a modest win, because its lead is measured in votes.
  • Size on the map still isn't people: a big pale county and a small dark one are drawn as big as their land.

So read the two together. Percentage color says how a county split; vote-lead color says how much that split counted. Use the slider to go back and forth.
