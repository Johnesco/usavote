Three ways to color a county map · 2026 U.S. Senate

Shade by votes, not by percentages

The usual election map shades each county by its percentage: the bigger the win, the darker the color. That makes a county where a hundred people voted look just as decisive as a big city. Every state map on this site has three choices under Color: Vote share, the usual map; Vote lead, shaded by how many votes each county's lead was worth; and Vote mix, each county's Democratic and Republican votes mixed from blue through purple to red, drawn fainter where few people voted. Here is what each one shows, and what each one hides.

1

Vote share: the usual map

Each county is colored for whoever won it, and shaded by the winner's share: light for a close race, dark for a landslide.

2

Where it fails: a tiny landslide looks bigger than a city

A small county that votes one way almost unanimously gets the darkest shade, darker than a big city that won by a huge number of votes.

3

Where it fails: dark ink, few votes

The darkest counties are mostly the emptiest. They cover a lot of the map but cast few of its votes.

4

Where it fails: a big, close county looks like nothing

A close race gets the lightest shade, even when the county is so big that a narrow win is worth tens of thousands of votes.

5

Vote lead

Now each county is shaded by its lead in votes: the winner's votes minus the runner-up's. The more votes a county added to its winner's statewide total, the darker it is.

The shading is on a square-root scale, the way a circle's area is scaled on a bubble map, so a county with a quarter of the biggest lead is half as dark. Without it, every county but a handful would be white.

6

Now the city outweighs the tiny landslide

The small county fades out, and the city is among the darkest on the map.

7

The empty counties fade

The counties that were the darkest red now barely show, because together they added few votes.

8

What Vote lead hides

No color scale shows everything. This one leaves out how a county split.

  • A pale county may still have voted 9 to 1. It just didn't have many voters. Press Vote share to see its percentage.
  • A big county can be dark with a modest win, because its lead is measured in votes.
  • Size on the map still isn't people: a big pale county and a small dark one are drawn as big as their land.

Vote share says how a county split; Vote lead says how much that split counted. The third color tries to show both at once.

9

Vote mix

Each county mixes its Democratic and Republican votes into one color, blue through purple to red, and is drawn fainter the fewer votes it cast.

Three choices keep this honest. The colors from blue through purple to red are all equally light and equally strong (they're picked in OKLCH, a color space made to match how people see), so an even county is a clear purple rather than a muddy one, and no split looks darker or louder than another. That leaves darkness free to mean one thing only: few voters. And no county fades away entirely: the smallest is still 40% solid, so you can still see its color. How solid a county is goes on a square-root scale, like Vote lead's. Other candidates' votes are left out of the mix. Read it with the key: the split across, votes cast down.

10

The tiny landslide keeps its color, but faintly

The small county still shows which way it went, but faintly; the city shows at full strength.

11

The big, close county shows as close, and as big

A close race in a big county is purple at nearly full strength: a narrow win, among a lot of voters.

12

Which one to use

  • Vote share shows how each county split. It hides how many people voted, so empty land looks as decisive as a city.
  • Vote lead shows how many votes each county added to its winner's total. It hides how a county split.
  • Vote mix shows the split as a color, from blue through purple to red, and the votes cast as how solid that color is. It leaves out other candidates, and it is more to read, so check the key.

On the state maps, Vote mix is for the flat map. In 3D, each column's height already shows the votes cast, so the colors go back to Vote share. Hover over any county, here or on a state map, for its exact numbers.
